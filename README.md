# MakesYouFluent – Language Learning Study Plan

A practical framework for setting language goals, organising weekly study, practising communication, and measuring progress.

## Purpose

A language study plan should answer four questions:

1. What do I need to learn?
2. Which skills need the most attention?
3. What will I practise this week?
4. How will I know whether I am improving?

This repository provides a structured way to answer them. It includes a planning method, sample schedules for different proficiency levels, progress measures, and reusable templates.

The framework can be adapted to different languages, learning goals, and available study time.

## 1. Define the Outcome

Start with a situation in which you want to use the language. Avoid goals that measure activity without measuring ability.

For example, completing 20 lessons is a study target. Being able to introduce yourself, ask questions, and maintain a short conversation is a communication target.

Use this format:

**I want to be able to [specific task] in [situation] by [target date].**

Examples:

| Learning goal | Evidence of progress |
|---|---|
| Handle basic travel conversations | Ask for directions, order food, and answer routine questions |
| Improve workplace communication | Explain a task and ask for clarification |
| Follow spoken conversations | Identify the main points and relevant details |
| Read everyday material | Understand messages, instructions, and short articles |
| Improve writing | Write a clear email or organised paragraph |
| Speak more comfortably | Maintain a conversation with fewer long pauses |

Choose one primary outcome and up to two supporting goals. This keeps the study plan focused.

## 2. Assess Your Current Ability

Assess each skill separately. A learner's reading ability may be considerably stronger than their speaking ability.

| Skill | What to assess |
|---|---|
| Listening | Can you understand the main idea and important details? |
| Speaking | Can you explain ideas and respond without relying on prepared sentences? |
| Reading | Can you understand the meaning and structure of a text? |
| Writing | Can you communicate clearly using appropriate vocabulary and grammar? |
| Vocabulary | Can you recognise and use relevant words in context? |
| Pronunciation | Can other speakers understand you clearly? |

Use short tasks to establish a starting point:

- Listen to a short conversation and summarise it.
- Speak for two minutes about a familiar subject.
- Read a short passage and answer questions.
- Write a paragraph without translating every sentence.
- Explain the meaning of recently learned expressions.

Record the results. Keep the original exercises so you can compare them with later attempts.

### Use CEFR as a reference

The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) provides proficiency levels from Pre-A1 and A1 through to C2.

Its descriptors focus on what learners can do with a language. Use them to identify suitable tasks and describe progress, rather than treating a level label as a complete assessment of every skill.

Reference: [Council of Europe – CEFR descriptors](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-descriptors)

## 3. Use the MakesYouFluent Weekly Planning Framework

The planning method has five steps:

**Set → Allocate → Practise → Review → Adjust**

### Step 1: Set the weekly target

Choose one communication task to work towards.

Example: Explain your daily routine and answer follow-up questions.

### Step 2: Allocate study time

Divide the available time between listening, speaking, reading, writing, and vocabulary review. Give extra attention to the weakest skill without neglecting the others.

### Step 3: Practise actively

Use the language. Answer questions, describe situations, write original sentences, and take part in conversations.

### Step 4: Review the results

Check what you could do independently, where you needed help, and which mistakes appeared repeatedly.

### Step 5: Adjust the next week

Keep activities that address genuine weaknesses. Replace tasks that are too easy, too difficult, or unrelated to your goal.

This is a planning framework, not a prescribed teaching method. The schedule should change as the learner's needs change.

## 4. Build a Weekly Schedule

The following 30-minute schedule provides a starting point.

| Day | Activity | Intended result |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Listening and vocabulary | Understand a short recording and identify useful expressions |
| Tuesday | Speaking | Describe a familiar subject and answer questions |
| Wednesday | Reading | Understand a short text and summarise its main points |
| Thursday | Grammar and writing | Apply a grammar point in original sentences |
| Friday | Conversation | Practise responding and asking follow-up questions |
| Saturday | Review and correction | Revisit difficult language and correct recurring errors |
| Sunday | Flexible practice | Use the language through an activity of your choice |

A missed session does not require doubling the next one. Resume the schedule and keep the workload manageable.

## 5. Sample Plans by Proficiency Level

These examples show how activities can change as learners develop. They are starting points, not fixed requirements.

### Beginner: Pre-A1 to A2

**Main priority:** Build the language needed for familiar, everyday situations.

| Activity | Example |
|---|---|
| Listening | Short introductions and everyday exchanges |
| Speaking | Introduce yourself, describe your routine, ask simple questions |
| Reading | Short messages, signs, menus, and simple passages |
| Writing | Basic descriptions, personal information, and short messages |
| Vocabulary | Common words and useful expressions grouped by situation |
| Review | Recall expressions and use them in new sentences |

**Weekly task:** Introduce yourself, describe where you live, and answer five questions about your daily routine.

### Intermediate: B1 to B2

**Main priority:** Communicate independently, explain opinions, and handle less familiar situations.

| Activity | Example |
|---|---|
| Listening | Interviews, discussions, and longer conversations |
| Speaking | Explain an opinion and support it with reasons |
| Reading | Articles, reports, and more detailed everyday material |
| Writing | Structured emails, summaries, and short arguments |
| Vocabulary | Collocations, topic-specific expressions, and natural phrasing |
| Review | Identify recurring grammar and communication problems |

**Weekly task:** Discuss a current topic, explain your position, and respond to questions or alternative viewpoints.

### Advanced: C1 to C2

**Main priority:** Improve precision, flexibility, register, and control in complex communication.

| Activity | Example |
|---|---|
| Listening | Complex discussions, lectures, and specialist material |
| Speaking | Present an argument and respond to challenging questions |
| Reading | Detailed articles, professional material, and nuanced texts |
| Writing | Analytical reports, persuasive writing, and detailed correspondence |
| Vocabulary | Nuance, idiomatic expressions, and register |
| Review | Examine precision, organisation, tone, and recurring errors |

**Weekly task:** Deliver a short presentation on a complex subject, answer follow-up questions, and review the clarity and precision of your responses.

## 6. Practise All Four Skills Together

Listening, speaking, reading, and writing support different parts of communication. A useful study session can connect them through one subject.

For example, a learner studying the topic of work could:

1. Read a short article about a workplace situation.
2. Listen to a related conversation.
3. Record unfamiliar expressions.
4. Explain the situation aloud.
5. Write a short response or summary.

This creates a connection between understanding language and using it.

Do not assume that strength in one skill automatically means equal ability in another. Track each skill separately.

## 7. Make Vocabulary Practice More Effective

A vocabulary list is useful only if learners return to the words and learn to use them.

For each new expression, record:

- The word or phrase.
- Its meaning in the current context.
- A natural example sentence.
- A sentence of your own.
- Whether you can recall and use it without help.

Example:

| Expression | Example | Your own use |
|---|---|---|
| Make a decision | We need to make a decision today. | |
| On the other hand | On the other hand, the alternative is less expensive. | |
| Look forward to | I look forward to meeting the team. | |

### Use spaced review

Return to new material across separate sessions instead of concentrating all repetition in one sitting.

One practical review schedule is:

- First review: the following day.
- Second review: three days later.
- Third review: one week later.
- Fourth review: two weeks later.

These intervals are a convenient starting schedule, not a universal formula. Review sooner when recall is difficult and extend the interval when the material is secure.

Whenever possible, try to recall the meaning before looking at the answer. Then use the expression in a new sentence or conversation.

## 8. Turn Feedback into Practice

Feedback should lead to another attempt.

After a speaking or writing exercise, record the most important corrections.

| Issue | Correction | Next practice |
|---|---|---|
| Incorrect verb tense | Review the correct form | Describe yesterday's activities |
| Unclear pronunciation | Identify the difficult sound | Practise it in several words and sentences |
| Limited vocabulary | Learn a more suitable expression | Use it in an original response |
| Word order problem | Review sentence structure | Write five new examples |
| Long pauses | Practise retrieving familiar expressions | Answer timed questions |

Do not try to correct every error in one session. Select two or three issues that affect clarity or occur frequently.

For speaking, repeat the original task after reviewing the corrections. For writing, produce a revised version without simply copying the corrected text.

## 9. Measure Progress Through Performance

Time spent studying and lessons completed can show consistency. They do not, by themselves, demonstrate improved communication.

Use repeatable tasks to assess ability.

| Skill | Progress measure |
|---|---|
| Listening | Correctly identify the main points in comparable recordings |
| Speaking | Explain a subject and respond to follow-up questions |
| Reading | Summarise a text and identify supporting details |
| Writing | Produce a clear response with fewer recurring errors |
| Vocabulary | Recall and use expressions without prompts |
| Pronunciation | Speak clearly enough to be understood in the task |

Repeat similar tasks every four weeks. Compare the recordings, written work, comprehension results, and corrections.

Keep the difficulty reasonably consistent when comparing results. A much easier exercise does not provide a reliable measure of improvement.

## 10. Adjust the Plan to Available Time

The same framework can work with different schedules.

### 15 minutes per day

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 5 minutes | Vocabulary recall |
| 5 minutes | Listening |
| 5 minutes | Speaking or sentence production |

### 30 minutes per day

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 10 minutes | Listening or reading |
| 10 minutes | Speaking or writing |
| 5 minutes | Vocabulary review |
| 5 minutes | Corrections |

### 45–60 minutes per day

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 15 minutes | Listening or reading |
| 15 minutes | Speaking or writing |
| 10 minutes | Vocabulary and grammar |
| 5–20 minutes | Feedback, corrections, and targeted practice |

These schedules are examples. Learners should adjust the balance to their goals and current abilities.

At the end of each four-week period, record:

- Tasks you can now complete independently.

- Areas that need a different practice approach.
- The next communication goal.

## 12. Common Planning Errors

**Setting goals that are too broad**

Replace "improve my English" with a task that can be practised and assessed.

**Studying only what feels comfortable**

A learner who enjoys reading but avoids speaking may need to allocate more time to conversation.

**Repeating material without testing recall**

Looking at a word repeatedly is not the same as being able to retrieve and use it.

**Changing the plan every few days**

Allow enough time to complete a practice cycle and review the results before making major changes.

**Treating mistakes as a reason to stop**

Record recurring problems and use them to select the next exercise.

**Measuring activity instead of ability**

Track what you can understand, explain, write, and communicate. Lesson counts are supplementary information.

## 13. Research and Further Reading

The planning framework draws on established work in language proficiency assessment and second-language learning. These sources provide background for the use of proficiency descriptors, spaced practice, and retrieval-based vocabulary review.

1. **Council of Europe – Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR), Companion Volume (2020).**  
   Provides proficiency descriptors and communicative activity scales for assessing what learners can do.  
   [Read the CEFR Companion Volume](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/cefr-companion-volume-and-its-language-versions)

2. **The Effects of Spaced Practice on Second Language Learning: A Meta-Analysis (2022).**  
   Reviews 48 experiments involving 3,411 participants and reports a medium-to-large overall effect of spaced practice on second-language learning.  
   [Read the research](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479)

3. **Improving Second Language Vocabulary Learning and Retention by Leveraging Memory Enhancement Techniques (2021).**  
   Examines the combination of spaced practice, retrieval practice, and feedback in second-language vocabulary learning.  
   [Read the research](https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688211053525)

4. **A Review of Laboratory Studies of Adult Second Language Vocabulary Training (2020).**  
   Reviews vocabulary-training methods and discusses the role of repetition, retrieval, spacing, and meaning-based practice.  
   [Read the review](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263119000500)

These studies address particular aspects of learning. They do not establish one ideal timetable for every learner or guarantee a specific rate of progress.

## Conclusion

A useful language study plan connects a clear communication goal with regular practice and measurable tasks.

Set a target. Give each skill appropriate attention. Use the language, review mistakes, and adjust the next week's work based on what the results show.

The plan should be specific enough to guide daily practice and flexible enough to change as proficiency develops.

## About MakesYouFluent

MakesYouFluent focuses on language learning and practical communication. This repository provides an independent planning resource for learners who want to organise their study, practise consistently, and assess their development.

## Scope

This repository is an educational resource, not a language proficiency test or a guarantee of learning outcomes. The schedules and templates are adaptable examples. Progress depends on factors including starting ability, language background, practice quality, feedback, and time invested.
